Can a simple rule beat buying the index?

Two monthly trading rules, momentum and mean reversion, tested on five Indian large-cap stocks against the Nifty 50 Price Index. Over this sample, neither beat the index.

The question

Can simple systematic strategies based on momentum and mean reversion outperform passive investing in Indian large-cap equities?

Momentum is the idea that recent winners keep winning for a while; mean reversion is the idea that recent losers bounce back. Both have a long history in finance research, mostly on US data over long periods. This project applies the plainest possible version of each to a small, current Indian sample, and reports what happened.

The hypotheses were set before looking at results, and none of them assumed the strategies would win: each strategy would produce a different cumulative return from the index (H1, H2), and might show different risk (H3).

Method

  • Signal. Each stock’s return in the previous month.
  • Momentum. Buy the two stocks with the highest signal.
  • Mean reversion. Buy the two stocks with the lowest signal: “buy whatever did worst last month”. Not a z-score or moving-average model.
  • Weights. 50/50 between the two stocks, rebalanced monthly.
  • No look-ahead. Month t’s selection uses only month t−1’s return. Checked for every stock and month: zero mismatches. August and September 2023 have no valid signal, so trading starts in October 2023.
  • Costs. The baseline assumes zero costs and slippage; costs are tested separately below.
  • Data. Monthly adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance via yfinance.

      Robustness

      Starting everyone at the same moment

      Because the strategies sit flat for their first two months while the index is already moving, all three were rebased to ₹100,000 at October 2023. The order does not change: Nifty ₹125,085.17, momentum ₹109,547.54, mean reversion ₹102,805.15.

        After the study: tracking the rules forward

        The paper’s results stop at June 2026 and do not change. Since then the same rules keep running on new prices, the same five stocks, the same two-stock picks, zero costs, so each new month is a test on data the rules have never seen.

          What it can and can’t show

          Within this five-stock universe and this period, the Nifty 50 Price Index accumulated more value than either rule. That is the finding, and it is worth stating plainly rather than explaining away.

          It does not show that momentum or mean reversion never work, or that active investing is pointless. It describes one small test, with one set of rules, over about three years.

          The paper connects momentum to possible investor underreaction and mean reversion to possible overreaction. Those are ideas from the wider literature offered as context. This backtest measures prices, not psychology, so it cannot say that either behaviour caused these returns.

          Limitations

          • Five stocks, a tiny slice of the market, held two at a time.
          • About three years of data, one historical window, no out-of-sample test.
          • Zero baseline costs and slippage.
          • The benchmark is a price index and excludes dividends, so it probably understates what passive investing returned.
          • Only a one-month signal was tried.

          Next

          Ideas from the paper, none of them tested yet: a wider universe (Nifty 100 or 500), longer lookbacks, a total-return benchmark, realistic cost data, and walk-forward testing on a period the rules have never seen.
