Inquiry/The Mathematics of Deception

Mathematics · probability and information theory

Can mathematics choose the next best question?

A painting disappears overnight. The watchman says he never left the east wing. Bayes’ theorem says how much each answer should change our belief; information theory says which question to ask next. This paper combines the two and tests them with a simulation.

The question

Given uncertainty about whether a statement is truthful, can Bayesian inference and information theory be combined to design a sequence of questions that maximises the probability of identifying the truth with the fewest questions?

It breaks into three parts: updating (how an answer should change the probability of a lie), measuring (how much uncertainty is left, and how much a question removes on average), and choosing (whether always asking the most informative question beats asking at random). Only the third makes a prediction that can be tested, and the paper tests it.

Not a lie detector. The paper is explicit that it does not detect lies in real people. It builds a small invented world with written-down rules, where every conclusion can be proved, and keeps that separate from real human behaviour.

The model

  • A hidden variable: the statement is a lie or it is true. The starting belief is 50/50.
  • Each question produces an observable feature (a hesitation, a contradiction, an oddly precise time). Liars and truth-tellers produce it with different, known probabilities.
  • Answers are independent given the truth, the suspect doesn’t adapt, and only a limited number of yes/no questions can be asked.

Every assumption is listed with the reason for it, and the later sections remove them one by one: different priors, wrong probabilities, correlated answers, a rehearsed liar, and a cost for each question.

Three ideas

Bayes’ theorem

Each answer multiplies the odds of a lie by its likelihood ratio, how much more often a liar gives that answer than a truth-teller.

Entropy

A measure of how uncertain we are: 1 bit at 50/50, zero at certainty.

Expected information gain

How much a question is expected to reduce entropy before we hear the answer. A question is only worth asking if liars and truth-tellers answer it differently. “Does he mention the weather?” (50% either way) is worth exactly nothing.

Try it: question the watchman

The computer has secretly decided whether the watchman is lying, like a coin toss. Your job is to find out with as few questions as you can.

  1. Ask a question. He answers the way the paper’s model says a liar or an honest person would, and neither is perfectly predictable.
  2. Watch the meter. It shows how likely a lie is, given every answer so far (Bayes’ theorem). Some questions move it a lot, some barely at all.
  3. Make your call when you’re confident, and see if you were right. The ★ marks the question the paper’s algorithm would ask next.

The ten questions, and how often liars and honest people give each answer, are the paper’s own question bank: modelling choices, not measurements of real people. The updates use the paper’s formulas.

So the strategy doesn’t find the truth more often than asking everything; about 88% is close to the limit of what the question bank contains. It finds it with far fewer questions, and when questions are scarce, it finds it more often.

The extensions show where this breaks. When the probabilities are wrong, when answers are correlated, or when the liar rehearses, the mathematics still runs but its confidence stops being trustworthy.

What it can’t do

  • Every probability in the question bank was invented. Measuring real ones would need careful experiments, and they would vary from person to person.
  • Real statements can be partly true, mistaken or misremembered. Nervousness is not deception.
  • The strategy is greedy: it picks one question at a time instead of planning the whole sequence.
  • The simulation tests the method against a world that follows the model’s rules exactly, not against reality.

The paper’s own summary: the mathematics does not detect lies. It detects uncertainty and reduces it.
