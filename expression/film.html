Everything Was Already Alive

Philosophical drama · Poetic realism · Slow cinema

A young man who feels disconnected from his own life begins filming people devoted to ordinary pursuits, hoping to understand what keeps them going. As their lives become material for his film, he has to face the difference between observing life and taking part in it.

Synopsis · no spoilers

Synopsis

In a coastal city that is never named, a young man in his early twenties lies on his bed in the afternoons and watches a rectangle of sunlight cross the wall. He lets full buses go past. His mother, who works long shifts at a pharmacy counter, has been told for three years that he is “thinking about it”. His oldest friend is leaving for a job in another city in six weeks.

Clearing out a cupboard, he finds a camcorder his mother owned “from before”. He starts to film the street, and then writes himself an instruction: find people who keep going — not famous, just going — and watch them.

He finds a painter in his seventies who keeps reworking one patch of blue on a canvas, a musician above a hardware shop who has been failing at the same note for nine years, and a dancer rehearsing alone in a rented hall. Then strangers: a florist who opens before sunrise, a bus driver near retirement, an old couple on a seafront bench. As the tapes pile up and he begins to shape them into a film, the question of what he is doing with their lives, and with his own, becomes harder to avoid.

The people

No one in the film has a name. They are known by what they do, or by what they are to him: the Protagonist, the Mother, the Friend, the Painter, the Musician, the Dancer, the Florist, the Bus Driver, the Old Couple, the Young Parent, the Vendor, the Widow.

Look and sound

Notes on the treatment, drawn from the screenplay’s own front matter.

Image

  • Early scenes frame him behind glass, in doorways, in reflections, at a distance. Muted colour, cool daylight.
  • As his project grows, his own footage becomes controlled: symmetrical, tripod-locked, perfectly lit. The film around him stays loose.
  • As he changes, the film’s camera relaxes: longer takes, warmer natural colour. The world does not change, only how it is held.
  • Two textures of image: the film’s own camera, and his footage (camcorder footage, on laptop screen), smaller, grainier, sometimes tilted.

Sound

  • Room tone, traffic, wind, the sea, a brush on canvas, strings, breathing.
  • A tap dripping into a steel basin in his flat, once every four seconds.
  • Music only when someone in the scene is making it.

Recurring motifs

  • A rectangle of sunlight crossing his bedroom wall every afternoon.
  • A patch of blue on an unfinished canvas.
  • A melody that is never played the same way twice.
  • A curtain moving in an empty room.
  • People trying to photograph the sky.
